# BTS Army Purple 2020

> Sixty-thousand purple stars in the dark — the Borahae fandom visual language as a complete design system.

## Origin

BTS's "I purple you" (보라해, Borahae) was coined by V (Kim Taehyung) at a November 2016 fan meeting, where he explained that purple is the last color of the rainbow — meaning "I will trust and love you for a long time." Within a year, the global Army fandom had built an entire visual identity around the phrase and its associated hex (#8A4FFF). By the 2018 Love Yourself world tour, the Army Bomb light-stick had become a visual lingua franca: sixty-thousand synchronized purple lights forming constellations in stadium darkness.

The post-2020 codification solidified specific design conventions: matte-black ceremony backdrops, pearl-white concert tickets and lyric cards, bilingual Korean-English typography, holographic lavender glows, and gold reserved exclusively for milestone celebrations. This is not HYBE's corporate purple — it is the Army-specific Borahae purple, born from fan communities across Seoul, São Paulo, Manila, Jakarta, and Mexico City, refined through millions of fan-made graphics into a coherent visual system.

## Colors

The palette is a concert encore frozen in time. Borahae purple (#8A4FFF) owns the page — it is the sky, the atmosphere, the sixty-thousand-light stadium. Pearl white (#F8F6F2) is the card surface, the lyric quote, the ticket — never the background. Matte black (#0F0F0F) provides ceremony weight. Holographic lavender (#C6A8FF) is the Army Bomb glow, used for subtle accents and hover states. Gold (#D9B14A) appears only at milestone moments — awards, anniversaries, chart achievements. The pink-purple (#B27AE0) bridges the primary and accent, used for secondary interactive elements.

## Typography

The type voice is bilingual, intimate, and concert-scale. Pretendard — Korea's post-2020 design-industry standard — handles headlines in both Korean and English with geometric clarity and tight tracking. Noto Sans KR provides reliable Korean body text, while Inter handles English body copy with matching x-height. Caveat appears exclusively for handwritten lyric-card quotes, evoking the fan-made graphic tradition of hand-lettered song lines shared on social media.

## Iconography

Icons are linear and lightweight — they should never compete with the purple atmosphere or lyric typography. Lucide provides the geometric clarity that matches Pretendard's character, with 1.5px stroke weight maintaining visibility on both purple and pearl surfaces.

- Treatment: linear (outline only)
- Set: Lucide
- Stroke: 1.5px
- Color: inherits from text context (eggplant on pearl, pearl-white on purple)

## Do's & Don'ts

### ✓ Do
- Use Borahae purple (#8A4FFF) as the dominant page atmosphere — it is the sky, not an accent
- Reserve gold (#D9B14A) exclusively for milestones, awards, and celebrations
- Pair Korean and English text in bilingual layouts — this is a transnational fandom
- Use Caveat only for lyric quotes and handwritten-feeling moments
- Let pearl-white cards float on purple with lavender glow shadows

### ✗ Don't
- Use generic teen-girl pink-and-glitter palettes
- Include K-pop "cute mascot" cartoon illustrations
- Use random K-drama subtitle-fonts as "Asian style"
- Center text with sparkle emoji decoration
- Apply Disney-saturated rainbow palettes
- Use holographic foil as full-page wallpaper (holographic is edge-accent only)
- Substitute stock concert-crowd photography for real Army content
- Confuse HYBE corporate-purple with Army Borahae purple
- Use Comic Sans or other novelty fonts for English lyrics
- Apply heavy serif fonts on Korean body type

It excels anywhere bilingual Korean-English content meets celebratory purple atmosphere — album release microsites, streaming milestone trackers, and fan-art gallery layouts. The 1:1 lyric-card format is native to Instagram and Twitter/X sharing.
