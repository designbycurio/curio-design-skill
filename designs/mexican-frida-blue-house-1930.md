# Mexican Frida Blue House (Coyoacán 1930)

> Cobalt-walled colonial courtyard packed with terracotta, bougainvillea, marigolds, and the emotional archive of Frida Kahlo's life.

## Origin

Casa Azul at Londres 247 in Coyoacán was built by Frida Kahlo's father Guillermo in 1904. Frida was born there in 1907, grew up inside its thick colonial walls, and would live, paint, suffer, and die in the house over the next forty-seven years. In the 1930s Diego Rivera codified the exterior by painting it the brilliant cobalt blue it retains today — a blue drawn from pre-Hispanic tradition and Catholic devotional architecture. The courtyard filled with terracotta pots, marigold beds, pre-Hispanic stone idols, papel-picado banners, retablo ex-votos, and Tehuana textiles from Oaxaca.

After Frida's death in 1954, Rivera donated the house to the Mexican people. It opened as the Museo Frida Kahlo in 1958, preserved as she left it — her wheelchair at the easel, her Tehuana dresses in the wardrobe, her collection of retablos on the walls. Hayden Herrera's 1983 biography *Frida: A Biography of Frida Kahlo* and Tina Modotti's photographic documentation of the house and its inhabitants cemented Casa Azul as an icon of Mexicanidad — not a museum of quiet contemplation, but a living, saturated, emotionally overwhelming domestic space. The aesthetic is colonial architecture at maximum chromatic intensity.

- **Layout**: Grid — cobalt-wall rectangular panels containing cream content blocks, echoing the house's thick-walled rooms and courtyard alcoves
- **Content width**: Container (1280px max) — generous but bounded, like the courtyard walls framing the sky

- **Grid intensity**: Strong — the grid is the architecture itself, bold and structural like the blue walls dividing courtyard from rooms

## Colors

The palette is extracted directly from the Casa Azul courtyard at golden hour. Cobalt blue (`#2058A8`) dominates as the page background — the famous blue walls — and is not a tint or accent but the primary atmospheric color of every screen. Cream (`#F0E5C8`) serves as the content surface, recalling uncoated paper and lime-washed interior walls. Terracotta orange (`#C85A2A`) provides the secondary voice — floor tiles, pot rims, thick card borders. Bougainvillea magenta (`#D8287A`) is the primary action color, hot and alive. Jade-leaf green (`#3A8C5A`) accents success states and botanical elements. Saffron yellow (`#E0A828`) and Tehuana red (`#A82428`) appear sparingly as warning and danger semantics.

## Typography

The typographic voice pairs Frida's raw diary handwriting energy with classical Mexican editorial refinement. Caveat provides heading text with its informal, diaristic cursive — the same urgent handlettered quality found in Frida's personal journals and retablo inscriptions. Cormorant Garamond handles body text with high-contrast elegance, connecting to the Spanish-language literary tradition and the serif typography of mid-century Mexican cultural publications. Cormorant SC supplies editorial small-caps. Marcellus provides cartouche lettering for framing elements. All type feels personal and warm — written by hand or set in hot metal, never extruded from a tech-company type system.

## Do's & Don'ts

### ✓ Do
- Use cobalt blue (#2058A8) as the dominant page background on every screen
- Frame cream content panels with thick terracotta (#C85A2A) borders
- Use bougainvillea magenta (#D8287A) for primary interactive elements
- Let Caveat headings feel handwritten and personal, like Frida's diary
- Keep the palette loud and saturated — this is a house of maximum chromatic intensity

### ✗ Don't
- Use white as the page background — cobalt is the dominant
- Fall back to cream/beige page backgrounds — cobalt is the right loud dominant
- Use modern sans-serif fonts (Inter is forbidden)
- Apply generic Mexican-folk pastel palettes
- Resort to tourist sombrero kitsch imagery
- Sanitize the aesthetic into a Pixar-Coco cartoon
- Apply slick corporate gallery branding
- Use subdued, muted tones — Frida is loud and saturated

## Applications

Casa Azul's aesthetic is ideal for cultural institution websites, art gallery portfolios, editorial magazines with a Latin American focus, event pages for folk-art exhibitions, and personal creative portfolios that demand maximum chromatic personality. The cobalt-dominant page paired with cream content panels creates an instantly recognizable visual signature that works across landing pages, article layouts, and image-heavy gallery grids.
