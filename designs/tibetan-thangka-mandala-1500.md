# Tibetan Thangka Mandala (1500)

> Sacred cosmological architecture rendered as interface — lapis indigo, mineral vermilion, gold-leaf elevation, and mandala symmetry.

## Origin

Tibetan thangka mandala painting reached its zenith between the 14th and 17th centuries in the monastic ateliers of Lhasa, Shigatse, and the Kham region. Two rival schools defined the tradition: the Menri school, founded by Menla Dondrup in the 15th century with its emphasis on precise iconometric measurement and mineral-saturated color, and the Karma Gardri school championed by Chöying Gyatso, which introduced subtler atmospheric effects while maintaining canonical proportions. The 10th Karmapa Chöying Dorje further expanded the tradition's reach across Bhutan and Mongolia. David Jackson's *A History of Tibetan Painting* remains the definitive scholarly survey of these lineages.

Each mandala is a cosmological blueprint: concentric rings of fire, vajra fences, and lotus petals enclose a palace square with four cardinal gates, converging on an inner sanctum housing the presiding deity. Every measurement — from petal count to deity proportion — is dictated by canonical texts. The palette is geological: lapis lazuli ground to deep indigo, cinnabar heated to vermilion, malachite crushed to green, and gold leaf burnished to luminous radiance. A single mandala could take a master painter two to six months to complete on sized cotton canvas.

## Colors

The palette is geological, not digital. Every hue maps to a mineral pigment ground by hand in a monastic atelier: lapis lazuli yields the deep indigo page ground, cinnabar produces the vermilion of lotus petals and primary actions, malachite gives the protective-ring green, and beaten gold leaf provides elevation and sanctity. Saffron orange marks inner-sanctum bands and focus states. The conch-white `#F2E9D8` is not a bright white — it carries the warm tint of actual conch shell, used sparingly for highlights and text on dark grounds.

Elevation is expressed as gold-leaf radiance rather than neutral drop shadows. The shadow ladder uses `rgba(212, 169, 56, ...)` — a warm gold glow that simulates the luminous halo around gilded deity figures. Higher-elevation elements appear to radiate light from within, like gold-leaf catching lamplight in a dim monastery hall. The deepest shadows use `rgba(26, 39, 82, ...)` — the indigo of the lapis ground itself — for inset and ambient depth.

- Surface: layered — cards and panels sit on distinct depth planes like concentric mandala rings
- Blur: none — mineral-pigment surfaces are matte, not frosted
